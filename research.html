How particles shape the flows that build the deep sea

My research links grain-scale sediment properties to the behavior of turbidity currents, the deposits they leave behind and the carbon they bury, with direct applications to reservoir prediction and carbon storage.

The big picture

Turbidity currents are sediment-laden underwater flows that carry sand, mud, nutrients and carbon from shallow seas into the deep ocean. Their deposits form some of the world's most important hydrocarbon reservoirs and are increasingly considered for subsurface carbon storage.

Most of what we know about these flows comes from siliciclastic (quartz-rich) systems. Carbonate and mixed carbonate–siliciclastic flows behave differently because their grains vary widely in shape, density and size. My work asks how those particle characteristics change flow structure, transport efficiency and deposit texture, and what that means for predicting reservoir quality.

Why it mattersMany models of these flows are based on quartz sand. Carbonate grains are often lighter, flatter or more irregular, so the same rules may not hold. Measuring the differences helps improve how we predict where good reservoir rock ends up

Doctoral dissertation

Mixed sediment gravity flows: effects of particle characteristics on flow structure, deposit texture and reservoir quality in underwater systems

Research themes

Three connected questions

1 · Flow dynamics

How do particle composition, shape and sediment concentration control the structure and run-out of turbidity currents? I compare flows of natural carbonate, quartz and mixed sands and muds under controlled laboratory conditions.

2 · Deposits & reservoir quality

How do those flow differences translate into grain sorting, bed texture and stacking patterns, and ultimately into porosity, permeability and connectivity in deepwater reservoirs?

3 · Carbon burial

How efficiently do carbonate turbidity currents move organic and inorganic carbon into the deep sea, and how should that change estimates of global carbon burial?

Recent findings

Carbonate turbidity currents and the carbon cycle

Using natural carbonate lagoon sediments in a series of flume experiments at increasing sediment concentrations, my collaborators and I showed that carbonate turbidity currents transport both organic and inorganic carbon, and partition them differently along the flow path.

Organic carbon was carried farther down-current, while inorganic carbon concentrated closer to the source. These patterns are similar to those reported from the Great Bahama Bank slope, which suggests carbonate turbidity currents may play a larger role in long-term carbon burial than is generally assumed.

Carbonate turbidity currents are worth a closer look in carbon-burial estimates.

Why it mattersThese are laboratory results, but they point to a process that is rarely included in carbon-burial estimates and is worth testing further in the field

Methods

From the lab bench to the basin

Experimental sedimentology

  • Flume experiments with natural carbonate, quartz and mixed sediments, including a pseudo-3D flume at Utrecht University
  • Velocity profiling and siphon sampling of flows at proximal and distal locations
  • Grain-settling column experiments and grain size and shape analysis, including laser diffraction

Subsurface characterization & modeling

  • Seismic interpretation, geological mapping and core logging
  • 3D geological modeling and uncertainty assessment in Petrel and RMS
  • Quantitative analysis and automation in Python and MATLAB

Collaborators

Geology Center of Research Excellence (CoRE) at the Colorado School of Mines; Utrecht University; and Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam.

Applications

Why it matters

Energy

Better predictions of where reservoir-quality sands occur in deepwater systems, and how well they connect, reduce exploration and development risk.

Carbon storage

Understanding deposit texture and heterogeneity helps identify and characterize subsurface units that can safely store CO₂.

Earth systems

Quantifying how sediment gravity flows move carbon refines our picture of the long-term global carbon cycle.
